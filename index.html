Border defence across more than 2,000 years

Great Wall of China

It was never one wall. It was a system of walls, towers, signals and soldiers that changed with every dynasty that used it.

Many walls, many builders

The wall people visit today is only the latest layer. Earlier states and dynasties built, linked, repaired and abandoned walls over many centuries.

  1. About 481 to 221 BCEWarring States

    Each of the seven states had defensive walls along its borders. After taking power, Qin Shi Huang destroyed these. (World History Encyclopedia)

  2. Qin dynastyThe northern wall

    Qin Shi Huang ordered a great wall along the northern border to defend against the Xiongnu and to show that China was unified. Unwilling conscripts and convicts built it under guard. It ran further north than the wall standing today. (World History Encyclopedia)

  3. 141 to 87 BCEHan under Emperor Wudi

    The wall was strengthened against the Xiongnu. In 121 BCE a 20-year project began on the Hexi Wall, from Yongdeng in Gansu to Lake Lop Nur in Xinjiang. It also helped regulate trade on the Silk Road. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)(World History Encyclopedia)

  4. 386 to 577Northern dynasties

    In 417 the Northern Wei built a segment over 1,000 km long from Chicheng in Hebei to Wuyuan in Inner Mongolia. In 555 the Northern Qi recruited 1.8 million workers for repairs and extensions. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)

  5. 581 to 618Sui

    The wall was repaired and improved seven times to defend against the Tujue. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)

  6. 1206 to 1368Yuan

    The Mongols saw little defensive value in the wall. Some forts and key areas were repaired and garrisoned to control commerce and limit rebellion. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)

  7. 1368 to 1644Ming

    The Ming strengthened the wall against another Mongol invasion, often along older Northern Qi and Northern Wei walls. They divided the border into nine defence zones. Most of the wall standing today is Ming work, and Britannica dates most of it to the reign of the Hongzhi emperor (1487 to 1505). (Britannica, Ming)

  8. After 1644Qing

    The Manchu border now lay north of the wall. A policy of reconciliation reduced repairs, and the wall gradually fell into ruin. (Britannica, Ming)(World History Encyclopedia)

How the Ming wall was built

Wall

The wall was built to suit the ground. On the Ming wall, the average height was 7 to 8 metres, about 6.5 metres wide at the base and 5.8 metres at the top, and a little lower on steep hills. Materials depended on the place: tamped earth between wooden boards, adobe, brick and stone, or rock. Walls on plains and at important passes were tall and strong, while those on steep mountains were low and narrow. Figures vary between sources, and another gives a height of 16 to 26 feet (5 to 8 m).

A system, not just a barrier

A wall alone is easy to climb or bypass. Builders added strongpoints, signals, garrisons and rules about who could pass.

Layers of strongpoints

A Han record describes the spacing as a beacon every 5 li, a tower every 10 li, a fort every 30 li and a castle every 100 li. The Ming divided the border into nine defence zones, the Nine Border Garrisons, each under a post chief. Under Qin, 12 prefectures were set up along the wall. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)(Britannica, Ming)

Barracks and towns

In the Northern Qi period, repairs and new sections totalling about 1,500 km (900 miles) came with towns and barracks at intervals to garrison them. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)

Control of trade

The Han used the wall to regulate trade along the routes later called the Silk Road. Repairs under Liu Xiu in 38 CE helped centralise control of trade and travel. The Yuan garrisoned forts to control commerce. (World History Encyclopedia)(Britannica, Han to Yuan)

A statement of unity

Qin Shi Huang ordered his wall to defend against the Xiongnu and also to show that China was unified. (World History Encyclopedia)

What it was built from, and by whom

Builders used what the land offered. Mountain sections, plains and passes were all built differently.

Tamped earth

Earth packed in layers between wooden boards. West of the Garrison of Yulin the Ming wall was packed earth. (Britannica, Ming)(Google Arts & Culture)

Brick and stone

Large bricks and stones faced the pass ramparts, and eastern walls such as those near Badaling were often faced with stone. General Qi Jiguang mainly built brick and stone walls, and suggested a wall starting at Shanhai Pass, over 1,000 km long. (Britannica, Ming)(Google Arts & Culture)

Mixed construction

At the Garrison of Shanxi, packed earth formed the inner side and bricks the outer side. At the Garrison of Ji the core was lime soil or rubble between blue bricks, stone strips or blocks, with lime paste bonding the brick joints. (Google Arts & Culture)

Whatever was nearby

The Liaoning Wall used earth, stone and whatever else was available. In some places the wall was made of oak, or of pilings and planks. (World History Encyclopedia)(Britannica, Ming)(Google Arts & Culture)

The workforce

Qin's wall was built by unwilling conscripts and convicts under guard. In 555 the Northern Qi recruited 1.8 million workers for repairs and extensions. (World History Encyclopedia)(Britannica, Han to Yuan)

Where the wall did not help

The wall was only as strong as the soldiers holding it. In 1644 the Ming dynasty ended without the Manchus having to break it.

  1. April 1644Beijing falls

    Rebels led by Li Zicheng took the Ming capital. Ming general Wu Sangui left his stronghold at Ningyuan, the only major army between the Manchus and Beijing, and returned to fortify Shanhai Pass. (Wikipedia, Shanhai Pass)

  2. 27 May 1644Battle of Shanhai Pass

    Wu asked the Manchu leader Dorgon for help driving out the rebels. Dorgon instead asked him to work for the Qing, and Wu accepted. Their combined armies routed Li's army. (Wikipedia, Shanhai Pass)(World History Encyclopedia)

  3. June to November 1644The Qing take over

    Dorgon entered Beijing on 5 June, and the Shunzhi Emperor was enthroned as Emperor of China on 8 November. (Wikipedia, Shanhai Pass)

The lesson. When Wu left Ningyuan, all the territory outside the wall came under Qing control. The wall had not been beaten, but the army that defended it had gone. (Wikipedia, Shanhai Pass)

Myths to check

You can see it from space

NASA says the wall generally cannot be seen with the unaided eye from low Earth orbit, and is very hard to photograph from there. Its materials are about the same colour and texture as the land around it. It does show up in radar images. Astronauts say it cannot be seen with the naked eye from the Moon. (NASA)(Britannica, from space)

It is one wall

It is many walls from many states and dynasties. Qin Shi Huang first linked earlier walls, and later dynasties kept building. His wall ran further north than the one standing today. (World History Encyclopedia)(Live Science)

It is mostly ancient

Most of the wall standing today is Ming work from 1368 to 1644, and Britannica dates most of it to the reign of the Hongzhi emperor (1487 to 1505). The Qin wall ran further north than the one visitors see now. (Britannica, Ming)(World History Encyclopedia)

It always kept invaders out

The Mongol Yuan saw little defensive value in it, and in 1644 the Manchus crossed after a Ming general joined them. (Britannica, Han to Yuan)(Wikipedia, Shanhai Pass)

Terms

Beacon tower
A hilltop tower that sent signals by fire, lantern or smoke.
Crenellation
Raised blocks along the top of a wall with gaps between, for watching and shooting.
Garrison
The soldiers stationed in a fort, pass or defence zone.
Hexi Wall
The Han wall running from Yongdeng in Gansu to Lake Lop Nur in Xinjiang.
Li
A traditional Chinese unit of distance, used in the Han record of strongpoint spacing.
Parapet
A low protective wall along the edge of a walkway.
Pass
A fortified gate through the wall where roads crossed the border.
Tamped earth
Earth packed hard in layers, often between wooden boards. Also called rammed earth.
Xiongnu
The people of the northern steppe that the Qin and Han walls were built against.
